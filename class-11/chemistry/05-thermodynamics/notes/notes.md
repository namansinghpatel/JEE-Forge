## 🎯 Quick summary (the whole topic in a nutshell)

Thermodynamics is the **energy bookkeeping** of chemistry: how much heat a reaction releases or needs, and
whether it will happen at all.

**The seven things to really understand:**

1. **System & state functions.** $U, H, S, G$ depend only on the state (path cancels — this is why Hess's
   law works); $q$ and $w$ depend on the path.
2. **First law:** $\Delta U = q + w$, with $w = -P_{\text{ext}}\Delta V$ (IUPAC: expansion → $w$ negative).
3. **Enthalpy:** at constant pressure the heat is $\Delta H = \Delta U + \Delta n_g RT$ (count only gas
   moles). Exothermic $\Delta H < 0$, endothermic $\Delta H > 0$.
4. **Thermochemistry:** reaction enthalpy from formation enthalpies (**products − reactants**) or bond
   enthalpies (**broken − formed**); **Hess's law** lets you add/reverse/scale equations.
5. **Entropy & second law:** a process is spontaneous if $\Delta S_{\text{universe}} > 0$. Entropy rises:
   solid → liquid → gas, mixing, more gas moles.
6. **Gibbs energy:** $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$. Negative → spontaneous; this is the single
   system-only test. The crossover temperature is $T = \Delta H/\Delta S$.
7. **Equilibrium link:** $\Delta G^\circ = -RT\ln K$ — the bridge to equilibrium (and electrochemistry next
   year).

**The master trick (advanced):** identify the constraint first — **constant V → $\Delta U$**, **constant P →
$\Delta H$**, **isothermal → $\Delta U = 0$**, **adiabatic → $q = 0$** — and the problem usually collapses to
one equation. Reversible work is always the maximum.
