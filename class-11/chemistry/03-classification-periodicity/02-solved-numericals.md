# Classification & Periodicity — Solved Problems

> **35 worked problems**, split **Basic (10) · Medium (15) · Advanced (10)**. Periodicity is a
> **reasoning** chapter, so most problems ask you to *arrange, compare, predict or identify* — with a few
> true numericals ($Z_{\text{eff}}$, position-from-configuration, successive-IE group assignment). Every
> problem marks the **❓ Question** and **✅ Answer**; Medium and Advanced carry a **🧭 Strategy** block.

<!-- RENDERING: inside math use \cdot (not ·), \to for arrows, Z_eff, superscripts for configs. -->

> **Guiding idea:** nearly every answer follows from *size and effective nuclear charge* — smaller atom + higher $Z_{\text{eff}}$ → higher IE, more negative electron gain enthalpy, higher electronegativity, more non-metallic/acidic.

## 🟢 Basic (B1–B10)

<a id="b1"></a>
### B1

> ❓ **Question.** To which block does an element with configuration $[\text{Ne}]3s^2 3p^4$ belong?

**✅ Answer.** Last electron in $3p$ → **p-block** (it's sulfur, group 16).

---

<a id="b2"></a>
### B2

> ❓ **Question.** State the period and group of an element with $Z = 19$.

**✅ Answer.** $[\text{Ar}]4s^1$ → period **4**, group **1** (potassium).

---

<a id="b3"></a>
### B3

> ❓ **Question.** Which is larger: Na or Na⁺?

**✅ Answer.** **Na** — the cation Na⁺ has lost its $3s$ shell and feels more pull per electron, so it is smaller.

---

<a id="b4"></a>
### B4

> ❓ **Question.** Across period 3 (Na → Ar), does atomic radius increase or decrease?

**✅ Answer.** **Decreases** (nuclear charge rises, electrons enter the same shell).

---

<a id="b5"></a>
### B5

> ❓ **Question.** Down group 1 (Li → Cs), how does ionisation enthalpy change?

**✅ Answer.** **Decreases** (valence electron farther from the nucleus and more screened).

---

<a id="b6"></a>
### B6

> ❓ **Question.** Which element is the most electronegative?

**✅ Answer.** **Fluorine.**

---

<a id="b7"></a>
### B7

> ❓ **Question.** Name the block and series for an element filling $4f$ orbitals.

**✅ Answer.** **f-block**, the **lanthanoid** series.

---

<a id="b8"></a>
### B8

> ❓ **Question.** Is the oxide of sodium (Na₂O) acidic, basic or amphoteric?

**✅ Answer.** **Basic** (metallic oxide on the left of the period).

---

<a id="b9"></a>
### B9

> ❓ **Question.** How many elements are in period 4?

**✅ Answer.** **18** ($4s$, $3d$, $4p$ = 2 + 10 + 6).

---

<a id="b10"></a>
### B10

> ❓ **Question.** Which has the larger atomic radius: C or Si?

**✅ Answer.** **Si** (below C in group 14 → extra shell).

<a id="medium"></a>
## 🟡 Medium (M1–M15)

<a id="m1"></a>
### M1

> ❓ **Question.** Arrange in increasing atomic radius: Na, Mg, Al, Si.

> [!TIP]
> 🧭 **Strategy.** Same period → radius decreases left to right.

**Steps.** Order of $Z_{\text{eff}}$: Si > Al > Mg > Na, so radius is the reverse.

**✅ Answer.** Si < Al < Mg < Na.

---

<a id="m2"></a>
### M2

> ❓ **Question.** Arrange the isoelectronic ions by increasing radius: O²⁻, F⁻, Na⁺, Mg²⁺.

> [!TIP]
> 🧭 **Strategy.** Isoelectronic (10 e⁻ each) → more protons means smaller.

**Steps.** Protons: Mg²⁺(12) > Na⁺(11) > F⁻(9) > O²⁻(8).

**✅ Answer.** Mg²⁺ < Na⁺ < F⁻ < O²⁻.

---

<a id="m3"></a>
### M3

> ❓ **Question.** Why is the first ionisation enthalpy of boron **less** than that of beryllium?

> [!TIP]
> 🧭 **Strategy.** Compare which electron is removed and the stability of what's left.

**Steps.** Be is $2s^2$ (stable, filled $2s$); B is $2s^2 2p^1$ — the single $2p$ electron is higher in energy and easier to remove.

**✅ Answer.** B removes a loosely-held $2p$ electron, while Be has a stable filled $2s^2$, so IE(B) < IE(Be).

---

<a id="m4"></a>
### M4

> ❓ **Question.** Why is IE of oxygen **less** than that of nitrogen?

> [!TIP]
> 🧭 **Strategy.** Half-filled stability.

**Steps.** N is $2p^3$ (stable half-filled); O is $2p^4$ — removing one electron relieves pairing repulsion and gives the stable $2p^3$.

**✅ Answer.** N's half-filled $2p^3$ is extra stable, so IE(O) < IE(N).

---

<a id="m5"></a>
### M5

> ❓ **Question.** Arrange by increasing first ionisation enthalpy: Na, Mg, Al, Si, P, S.

> [!TIP]
> 🧭 **Strategy.** General increase across the period, with dips at Al (< Mg) and S (< P).

**Steps.** Base trend Na<Mg<Al<Si<P<S, corrected for Al<Mg and S<P.

**✅ Answer.** Na < Al < Mg < Si < S < P.

---

<a id="m6"></a>
### M6

> ❓ **Question.** Which has a more negative electron gain enthalpy: F or Cl? Why?

> [!TIP]
> 🧭 **Strategy.** F is very small → electron–electron repulsion in the compact $2p$.

**Steps.** Adding an electron to F's small $2p$ shell faces strong repulsion, reducing the energy released.

**✅ Answer.** **Cl** (more negative) — F's tiny size makes its electron gain enthalpy less negative than expected.

---

<a id="m7"></a>
### M7

> ❓ **Question.** Identify the element: period 3, group 16.

> [!TIP]
> 🧭 **Strategy.** Period 3 → $n=3$; group 16 → $3s^2 3p^4$.

**Steps.** Configuration $[\text{Ne}]3s^2 3p^4$, $Z = 16$.

**✅ Answer.** **Sulfur (S).**

---

<a id="m8"></a>
### M8

> ❓ **Question.** An element has configuration $[\text{Ar}]3d^{10}4s^2 4p^3$. Give its period, group and block.

> [!TIP]
> 🧭 **Strategy.** Period = largest $n$; p-block group $= 10 + (4s + 4p)$ electrons.

**Steps.** Largest $n = 4$; valence $4s^2 4p^3$ → group $10 + 5 = 15$; last electron in $4p$ → p-block.

**✅ Answer.** Period **4**, group **15**, **p-block** (arsenic).

---

<a id="m9"></a>
### M9

> ❓ **Question.** Arrange by increasing electronegativity: C, N, O, F.

> [!TIP]
> 🧭 **Strategy.** Increases left to right across period 2.

**Steps.** Same period, $Z_{\text{eff}}$ rises.

**✅ Answer.** C < N < O < F.

---

<a id="m10"></a>
### M10

> ❓ **Question.** Which element has the lowest ionisation enthalpy in period 3, and which the highest?

> [!TIP]
> 🧭 **Strategy.** Lowest at the left (Na), highest at the noble gas (Ar).

**Steps.** Na loses its single $3s$ electron easily; Ar has a stable octet.

**✅ Answer.** Lowest: **Na**; highest: **Ar**.

---

<a id="m11"></a>
### M11

> ❓ **Question.** Predict the nature (acidic/basic/amphoteric) of the oxides: Na₂O, Al₂O₃, SO₃.

> [!TIP]
> 🧭 **Strategy.** Metallic → basic; metalloid/intermediate → amphoteric; non-metallic → acidic.

**Steps.** Na (metal) → basic; Al (amphoteric metal) → amphoteric; S (non-metal) → acidic.

**✅ Answer.** Na₂O basic, Al₂O₃ amphoteric, SO₃ acidic.

---

<a id="m12"></a>
### M12

> ❓ **Question.** Which is bigger: Cl or Cl⁻? By what reasoning?

> [!TIP]
> 🧭 **Strategy.** Anion vs atom.

**Steps.** Cl⁻ has an extra electron → more electron–electron repulsion, same nuclear charge spread over more electrons.

**✅ Answer.** **Cl⁻** is larger than Cl.

---

<a id="m13"></a>
### M13

> ❓ **Question.** Name the most and least metallic elements among: Na, Mg, Al, K.

> [!TIP]
> 🧭 **Strategy.** Metallic character increases down a group and decreases across a period.

**Steps.** K is below Na (more metallic); Al is farthest right of period 3 here (least).

**✅ Answer.** Most metallic: **K**; least: **Al**.

---

<a id="m14"></a>
### M14

> ❓ **Question.** The successive ionisation enthalpies (kJ/mol) of an element are 738, 1451, 7733, 10543. Identify its group.

> [!TIP]
> 🧭 **Strategy.** A big jump signals breaking into the noble-gas core; electrons removed before the jump = valency.

**Steps.** Large jump between the 2nd (1451) and 3rd (7733) IE → 2 easily-removed electrons.

**✅ Answer.** **Group 2** (it's magnesium).

---

<a id="m15"></a>
### M15

> ❓ **Question.** Which has the higher second ionisation enthalpy: Na or Mg? Why?

> [!TIP]
> 🧭 **Strategy.** After the 1st electron, what core is being broken?

**Steps.** Na⁺ is $[\text{Ne}]$ (noble-gas core) — removing a 2nd electron is very hard; Mg⁺ still has a $3s^1$ electron, easy to remove.

**✅ Answer.** **Na** has the much higher 2nd IE (breaking a noble-gas core).

<a id="advanced"></a>
## 🔴 Advanced (A1–A10)

<a id="a1"></a>
### A1

> ❓ **Question.** Using Slater's rules, estimate $Z_{\text{eff}}$ felt by a valence ($3p$) electron in a sulfur atom ($Z = 16$, config $1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2 3p^4$).

> [!TIP]
> 🧭 **Strategy.** $\sigma$: same-group ($3s,3p$) electrons $\times 0.35$; $n=2$ electrons $\times 0.85$; $n=1$ electrons $\times 1.00$.

**Steps.** Same group ($3s^2 3p^4$ → 5 others) $= 5(0.35) = 1.75$; $n=2$ (8 electrons) $= 8(0.85) = 6.8$; $n=1$ (2) $= 2(1.0) = 2.0$. $\sigma = 10.55$; $Z_{\text{eff}} = 16 - 10.55$.

**✅ Answer.** $Z_{\text{eff}} \approx 5.45$.

---

<a id="a2"></a>
### A2

> ❓ **Question.** Why does $Z_{\text{eff}}$ increase across a period but the screening constant stays nearly constant?

> [!TIP]
> 🧭 **Strategy.** Electrons added go into the **same** shell, which shields poorly.

**Steps.** Each added proton raises $Z$ by 1; the added electron is in the same shell and screens the others by only ~0.35, so $Z_{\text{eff}} = Z - \sigma$ climbs.

**✅ Answer.** Same-shell electrons screen weakly (~0.35 each), so $Z$ outpaces $\sigma$ → $Z_{\text{eff}}$ rises.

---

<a id="a3"></a>
### A3

> ❓ **Question.** The successive IEs (kJ/mol) are 578, 1817, 2745, 11578. Identify the group and the element's likely identity in period 3.

> [!TIP]
> 🧭 **Strategy.** Jump after the 3rd IE → 3 valence electrons.

**Steps.** Big jump between 3rd (2745) and 4th (11578) → 3 removable electrons → group 13.

**✅ Answer.** **Group 13** (aluminium).

---

<a id="a4"></a>
### A4

> ❓ **Question.** Explain the diagonal relationship between lithium and magnesium with one shared property.

> [!TIP]
> 🧭 **Strategy.** Moving right (↑ charge density) ≈ cancels moving down (↓ charge density), so Li ~ Mg.

**Steps.** Both have similar charge/size ratios; e.g. both form **covalent, LiCl/MgCl₂-type** compounds, both nitrides ($\text{Li}_3\text{N}$, $\text{Mg}_3\text{N}_2$) form directly with N₂, and both carbonates decompose on heating.

**✅ Answer.** Li ~ Mg: both react with N₂ to form nitrides, and their carbonates decompose on heating (unlike other group-1 carbonates).

---

<a id="a5"></a>
### A5

> ❓ **Question.** Arrange by increasing size: Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺, F⁻, O²⁻, N³⁻.

> [!TIP]
> 🧭 **Strategy.** All isoelectronic (10 e⁻). Fewer protons → larger.

**Steps.** Protons: Al³⁺(13) > Mg²⁺(12) > Na⁺(11) > F⁻(9) > O²⁻(8) > N³⁻(7).

**✅ Answer.** Al³⁺ < Mg²⁺ < Na⁺ < F⁻ < O²⁻ < N³⁻.

---

<a id="a6"></a>
### A6

> ❓ **Question.** Predict the maximum oxidation state of an element in group 17 (e.g. chlorine), and explain.

> [!TIP]
> 🧭 **Strategy.** For high p-block groups, max oxidation state ≈ group number − 10.

**Steps.** Group 17 → max positive state $= 17 - 10 = +7$ (as in $\text{HClO}_4$, $\text{Cl}_2\text{O}_7$).

**✅ Answer.** **+7** (fluorine is an exception — only −1/0 due to no d-orbitals and highest electronegativity).

---

<a id="a7"></a>
### A7

> ❓ **Question.** Explain why the ionisation enthalpy of aluminium is lower than that of magnesium.

> [!TIP]
> 🧭 **Strategy.** Compare $3s^2$ vs $3p^1$ removal.

**Steps.** Mg is $3s^2$ (filled, stable); Al is $3s^2 3p^1$ — the single $3p$ electron is higher in energy and better screened, so easier to remove.

**✅ Answer.** Al loses an easily-removed $3p$ electron while Mg has a stable $3s^2$, so IE(Al) < IE(Mg).

---

<a id="a8"></a>
### A8

> ❓ **Question.** Among period-2 elements, which has the most positive electron gain enthalpy, and why?

> [!TIP]
> 🧭 **Strategy.** A stable filled/closed configuration resists gaining an electron.

**Steps.** Neon ($2s^2 2p^6$, noble gas) would have to place the electron in the next shell → energy is absorbed.

**✅ Answer.** **Neon** (positive $\Delta_{eg}H$); beryllium and nitrogen are also near-zero/positive due to filled $2s^2$ and half-filled $2p^3$.

---

<a id="a9"></a>
### A9

> ❓ **Question.** An element forms an oxide $\text{X}_2\text{O}_7$ and is a solid non-metal. Suggest its group and a likely identity.

> [!TIP]
> 🧭 **Strategy.** Highest oxide stoichiometry $\text{X}_2\text{O}_7$ → max oxidation state +7 → group 17.

**Steps.** Group 17, solid non-metal → iodine (or $\text{Cl}_2\text{O}_7$ for chlorine, but Cl is a gas). A solid points to iodine/its oxides.

**✅ Answer.** **Group 17** (halogen); a solid example is iodine.

---

<a id="a10"></a>
### A10

> ❓ **Question.** Compare the second ionisation enthalpies of Li and Be, and explain the large difference.

> [!TIP]
> 🧭 **Strategy.** After the 1st electron, is a noble-gas core being broken?

**Steps.** Li⁺ is $1s^2$ (He core) — removing a 2nd electron is extremely hard; Be⁺ is $1s^2 2s^1$ — the $2s^1$ is easy to remove.

**✅ Answer.** $\Delta_i H_2(\text{Li}) \gg \Delta_i H_2(\text{Be})$ because Li's 2nd electron comes from a stable He core.
